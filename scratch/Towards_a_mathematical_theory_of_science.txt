Towards a mathematical theory of science

1

Overview

We propose a formal framework for representing scientific discourse as trajectories over a semantic embedding space. The framework distinguishes carefully between:
(i) empirical objects constructed directly from data,
(ii) statistical structures inferred from empirical regularities,
(iii) and theoretical interpretations imposed on top of those structures.
The framework does not assume that embeddings directly represent the true geometry of
knowledge. Instead, embeddings are treated as noisy coordinate systems induced by statistical
regularities in scientific language.

Hypothesis Structure

The framework proposes three progressively stronger empirical hypotheses.
• H1: local discourse displacements exhibit non-random structure,
• H2: conditional transition distributions exhibit organized local geometry,
• H3: transition structure possesses predictive value for future semantic occupation patterns.
These hypotheses correspond respectively to:
(i) existence of structured transition dynamics,
(ii) organization of local transition landscapes,
(iii) temporal predictive utility.

Novelty and Closure Metrics

13.1

Cycle Closure Approximation

A complete epistemic cycle may be interpreted theoretically as a transformation:
Φ:P →P
mapping an initial problem state into a future problem state generated through scientific
reasoning.
Empirically, however, future problems are not directly observed within a single scientific
abstract. Therefore, we define a first-order empirical approximation based on the semantic
displacement between the initial problem embedding and the interpretation embedding.
For document i:
(i)

∆cycle = ii − pi
Since:
(ii − fi ) + (fi − mi ) + (mi − pi ) = ii − pi
this quantity represents the net open-cycle semantic displacement accumulated along the
observed discourse trajectory.

13.2

Closure Condition

We define an approximate closure condition:
||ii − pi || < ϵ
for some small threshold ϵ.
Intuitively, this corresponds to trajectories in which the interpretation semantically returns
near the original problem region.
Such trajectories may correspond to:
• confirmatory studies,
• replication experiments,
11

• local theoretical validation,
• or highly incremental scientific progress.
In contrast:
||ii − pi || ≫ 0
indicates trajectories whose interpretations diverge substantially from the original problem
framing.
These may correspond to:
• conceptual expansion,
• methodological transfer,
• paradigm generalization,
• or broader epistemic reframing.

13.3

Novelty Metric

We define the empirical novelty score:
N (i) = ||ii − pi ||
which measures the semantic displacement between the initial problem framing and the final
interpretation framing of a scientific trajectory.
Importantly, this metric does not measure truth, correctness, or scientific quality. Instead,
it approximates the degree of semantic and conceptual displacement induced by the discourse
trajectory.

13.4

Future Problem Approximation

A more complete approximation of epistemic closure may be constructed using conditional
transition distributions.
Let:
D(P | i)
be the empirical distribution of problem embeddings associated with semantic neighborhoods
around interpretation state i.
We define the expected future problem embedding:
p̄i = E[P | i]
which may be estimated operationally using nearest-neighbor averaging over problem embeddings associated with neighboring interpretation states.
This yields an approximate net epistemic displacement:
(i)

∆E = p̄i − pi
where:
(i)

||∆E ||
represents an estimate of the future problem displacement induced by the discourse trajectory.

Theoretical Interpretation Layer

The empirical framework described above constructs semantic representations from scientific
discourse without requiring that embedding geometry correspond directly to literal epistemic
structure. However, the observed regularities motivate a higher-level theoretical interpretation in which scientific activity is represented as a temporally evolving higher-order epistemic
structure.
Importantly, EDEL distinguishes between:
(a) an empirical ontology, in which semantic and topological structures are reconstructed from
scientific discourse data, and
(b) a generative ontology, in which these structures become operational substrates for synthetic epistemic agents capable of generating and composing scientific reasoning trajectories.

14.1

EDEL as a Layered Formal Structure

We define empirical EDEL as the pair:
Ê = (Ê, T̂ )
where:
• Ê defines the semantic embedding substrate,
• T̂ defines the temporally evolving epistemic-topological structure reconstructed from discourse.
The semantic embedding structure is defined as:
Ê = (E, ϕ)
where:
• E ⊆ Rd is the semantic embedding space,
• ϕ : Tdisc → E maps discourse-role segments into semantic coordinates.
Given a scientific artifact ai , segmentation produces discourse-role embeddings:
pi , mi , fi , ii ∈ E
corresponding respectively to problem, method, finding, and interpretation discourse.
Importantly, these embeddings are treated as empirical semantic representations rather than
direct epistemic states.

14.2

Discourse Simplices

Each scientific artifact induces a discourse simplex:
σi = (pi , mi , fi , ii )
interpreted geometrically as a 3-simplex embedded in semantic space.
Within this interpretation:
• vertices correspond to discourse-role embeddings,
13

• edges correspond to pairwise discourse transitions,
• triangular faces correspond to partial epistemic configurations,
• the tetrahedral simplex represents a complete discourse trajectory associated with a research approach.
Unlike conventional graph-based representations, discourse simplices preserve higher-order
relational structure between all discourse components simultaneously.

14.3

Epistemic Simplicial Complex

The global epistemic organization is represented as a temporally indexed simplicial complex:
T = {Kt }t∈T
where each:
Kt = (St , Dt )
contains:
• a set of discourse simplices:
St = {σi }
• a family of conditional transition operators:
Dt (Y | x), x ∈ X
defined over discourse spaces:
X, Y ∈ {P, M, F, I}
The transition operators define asymmetric semantic attachment structure between discourse regions.
For example:
D(M | p)
describes methods empirically associated with neighboring problem states, while:
D(P | m)
describes problem regions empirically associated with neighboring methods.
Importantly:
D(M | p) ̸= D(P | m)
in general.
The resulting structure therefore defines a directed higher-order epistemic topology rather
than a purely symmetric simplicial complex.

14

14.4

Empirical Interpretation

Within the empirical ontology, the simplicial complex is interpreted as a statistical reconstruction of scientific discourse organization.
In this setting:
• semantic geometry approximates discourse similarity,
• simplicial attachment approximates higher-order epistemic organization,
• transition operators approximate empirical semantic affordances between discourse regions.
Importantly, the framework does not claim that embeddings or simplices constitute literal
epistemic states. Rather, they provide computable approximations of structural regularities
observable in scientific discourse corpora.

14.5

Closure and Epistemic Expansion

The displacement:
∆E (i) = ii − pi
defines the net semantic displacement between the initial problem discourse and the final
interpretive discourse of a scientific artifact.
Its norm:
N (i) = ∥ii − pi ∥
is interpreted as an empirical approximation of epistemic expansion.
Small values correspond to semantically closed trajectories in which interpretive discourse
remains close to the original problem framing, while large values correspond to semantically
expansive trajectories generating displaced conceptual regions.
Higher-order geometric observables may also be defined over discourse simplices, including:
• edge lengths,
• face areas,
• simplex volumes,
• operator-space simplices constructed from displacement operators.
These quantities characterize increasingly complex forms of epistemic transformation geometry.

Within this ontology, scientific discovery is interpreted as growth and restructuring of the

Importantly, the category-theoretic layer is not directly observable empirically. Rather, it
provides a formal operational semantics for generative scientific systems built on top of empirically reconstructed epistemic topology.

15.1

Epistemic Value

The original Epistemic Landscape (EL) framework models scientific exploration as search over
a landscape whose topology represents research approaches and whose height corresponds to
their epistemic value. In EDEL, this idea is generalized from isolated points to higher-order
epistemic structures represented as discourse simplices.
Formally, let:
σi = (pi , mi , fi , ii )
denote the discourse simplex associated with scientific artifact i, where:
• pi is the problem discourse embedding,
• mi is the method discourse embedding,
• fi is the finding discourse embedding,

16

• ii is the interpretation discourse embedding.
We define the epistemic value operator:
V:S→R
where:
S = {σi }
is the set of discourse simplices composing the EDEL complex.
The quantity:
V(σi )
represents the epistemic utility associated with the corresponding scientific reasoning configuration. Importantly, epistemic value is treated as formally distinct from semantic geometry
and topological structure. A simplex may occupy a dense or central region of the epistemic
complex while still having low epistemic utility, and conversely highly valuable simplices may
emerge in sparse or weakly connected regions.
This distinction separates:
• the structure of scientific discourse,
• from the value of exploring particular epistemic regions.
Empirical EDEL In empirical EDEL, epistemic value cannot be observed directly and must
therefore be approximated through external scientometric or social indicators. Let:
V̂(σi )
denote an empirical estimator of epistemic value.
Examples include:
• citation counts,
• field-normalized citations,
• PageRank-style influence metrics,
• theorem reuse frequency,
• downstream clinical or technological adoption.
In the present work, we primarily consider:
V̂(σi ) = log(1 + ci )
where ci denotes the citation count associated with artifact i.
These quantities should not be interpreted as direct measures of truth or intrinsic scientific
merit. Instead, they provide imperfect observational proxies for the social and epistemic impact
of scientific artifacts. Epistemic value in empirical EDEL therefore remains partially latent and
institutionally mediated.

17

Generative EDEL In generative EDEL, epistemic value is not inferred from historical traces
but defined explicitly through the ground-truth structure of the target problem domain.
Let:
G
denote the ground-truth environment associated with a scientific domain or task. Epistemic
value is then defined as:
V :S ×G →R
assigning utility to discourse simplices according to their contribution toward solving or
explaining the target environment.
Examples include:
• predictive accuracy,
• theorem proving success,
• simulation performance,
• optimization objectives,
• explanatory compression,
• environment reward.
In this setting, epistemic value becomes operationally well-defined and externally grounded.
Scientific agents operating over the EDEL structure can therefore optimize exploration policies
relative to explicit utility functions while remaining constrained by the transition structure of
the epistemic complex.
Extended EDEL Formalism With the inclusion of epistemic value, the empirical EDEL
framework can be summarized as:
Ê = (Ê, T̂ , V̂)
where:
• Ê defines the semantic and topological epistemic structure,
• T̂ defines the epistemic transition dynamics,
• V̂ defines the epistemic valuation structure.
Including the compositional semantics layer on the generative ontology yields the extended
formulation:
E = (E, T , C, V)
where:
C
defines the compositional and categorical structure governing admissible epistemic operations and scientific reasoning processes.
